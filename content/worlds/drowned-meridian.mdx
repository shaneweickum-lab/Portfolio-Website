## The Rising

Forty years ago, the Meridian coastline rose eleven meters in eighteen
months. No war caused it, no prophecy predicted it — the water simply came,
and did not recede. The generation that survived the Rising built its new
world quite literally on top of the old one: cities on stilts above cities
on foundations, salvage economies built around diving the drowned streets
below.

This is a world defined less by magic than by memory — what got saved,
what got lost, and who gets to decide which version of history the next
generation inherits.

## The Tidewrit Concord

The Concord controls the only reliable record of what the coastline looked
like before the Rising — surveyed, catalogued, and guarded as fiercely as
any treasury. They sell access to that record: salvage rights, property
claims, even the "true" shape of a drowned family's ancestral home. Whether
the Concord's maps are accurate is a question nobody with power has an
incentive to ask.

## Ashgate Remnant

What's left of the old coastal government, still insisting it is the
legitimate authority over land that is, in most cases, now nine meters
underwater. The Remnant's power is almost entirely symbolic — but symbols
matter enormously to a generation that lost every physical thing it used to
point to and say "mine."

## The Uncounted

Everyone the Concord's ledgers don't have a category for: divers born after
the Rising with no pre-Rising deed to claim, salvage crews operating outside
any registered guild, and the growing population of people who simply
decided the old records shouldn't get to define the new world. Loosely
organized, increasingly influential, and the group every other faction
underestimates.

## Where the stories live

The Drowned Meridian is the setting for *Where the Rivers Forget*, and for
most of what I write when I want to think about memory, ownership, and what
survives a disaster that isn't anyone's fault.

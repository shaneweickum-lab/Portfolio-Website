Mira had dived the Corrando Street house eleven times before she noticed the
door was wrong.

Not wrong in the way everything down here was wrong — swollen, soft,
half-eaten by forty years of salt. Wrong in the way that made her stop
kicking and hang there in the green-black water, bubbles crawling up past
her mask, staring at a doorframe that did not match the one in the Concord's
survey.

The survey said single door, east-facing, brass fittings. This door was
double, and it faced south, and there were no fittings left to check because
there had never been brass fittings to begin with — she could see the ghost
of iron hinges instead, rusted down to lace.

She surfaced too fast. Bad habit, the kind that got divers her mother's age
retired early with lungs like wet paper. She didn't care. She sat on the
gunwale of the skiff with her mask pushed up into her hair and thought about
the fact that she had paid the Concord four months of salvage credit for
that survey. Four months, for a drawing of a house that did not exist.

"You're supposed to log the depth before you come up," said Toma, not
looking up from the winch.

"The door's wrong."

"Doors are always wrong. That's why they call it salvage and not shopping."

"No." She peeled off one glove, finger by finger, buying herself a second to
find the words. "The *house* is wrong. The survey doesn't match. Not
water damage — wrong. Different door, different wall, whole different
house wearing my grandmother's address."

Toma finally looked up. Whatever he saw in her face made him stop cranking
the winch.

"Then either the Concord's survey is wrong," he said slowly, "or the house
you grew up hearing about was never the house that was actually there."

Mira looked back down into the water, where the drowned street kept its
silence the way it always had, the way it was going to keep it no matter
which answer she chose to believe.

"I need to go back down," she said.

"Mira—"

"I need to see how wrong."
